## What October grouse hunting in ME/NH/VT actually needs (lens baseline, web-checked)

- **Fall cover differs from drumming cover.**
  - Fall birds sit in dense young hardwood and aspen regeneration, roughly 6–20 yr, often with a sapling stem density in the thousands per acre. They also use alder runs, old-field and apple edges, and softwood thermal cover next to food.
  - Drumming habitat is a related but different selection. Spring males pick logs in 8–20-yr aspen with high stem density (VT F&W Landowner's Guide; MI DNR).
  - The three-age-class interspersion inside a home range of about 40 acres (0–10, 10–25, 25+ yr) is the classic management target.
- **The labels are spring-heavy.** About 61% of eBird records are April–June, against about 15% for September–November (verified by B and E from GBIF facets). Drumming detection runs at about 0.33 per survey, is weather-driven, and drums carry 50 m and more. Any checklist model is a drumming-audibility map unless something stops it.
- **Fall dispersal ("shuffle").** In September–October juveniles disperse, so October use is not spring use.
- **Absolute scale.**
  - NH flush rate per hunter-hour was 1.31 in 2019 and 2.27 in 2020. North-region observation rates were 136–143 per 100 dog-hours in 2017–18.
  - The survey has only about 150 respondents per year, so the regional scorer is weak.
  - A's "unverified" 1.3–2.3/h is in fact consistent with these figures.
- **What a hunter actually uses.** Coverts of 2–40 ha, legal access (including Maine industrial gate land), walk-in distance, a GPX loop from parking, and freshness. Freshness means 2025–26 cuts, which no static map has.
- **Cycles and pressure.**
  - Regional-year swings do not change *ranking* within a season.
  - Hunting pressure depletes roadside public coverts by late October. Only A models pressure.

## Repository fact check: a new shared flaw that all rounds missed

**Legacy LANDFIRE layers have no vintage before 2022 on disk.**
- **Evidence:**
  - `grouse_data.py:373-375`: "LANDFIRE has nothing before 2022".
  - Report §2.4 point 2: v(c, 2020) = 2022.
  - TreeMap exists only for 2016/2020/2022(/2023).
  - `mch_*` and `road_dist` are static copies.
- **The conflict.** Every design feeds "the existing 15 layers + `mch_*`" (`diagnose_gbm_baseline.py` design) as features for EBD checklists from **2016–2025**, "read for the checklist's own year". For 2016–2019 checklists, that silently reads evt/evh/evc/sclass/fdist/ch/cc from **2022**, 3–6 years late. `grep -i vintage|YEAR_MIN` across round 3 finds no design that handles this.
- **Why it matters ecologically.** The error falls on exactly the axis a hunter cares about.
  - Take a 2017 checklist in a 2015 clearcut. It sees a 2-yr-old slash field in reality, but its features describe a 7-yr dense sapling stand, which is prime fall cover.
  - Labels and young-cover features are therefore misaligned for about 40% of the training years. The age response gets smeared toward older ages.
- **It also hits the panel tests** (C's X2; B5 in B; D's route 2; E's X2):
  - The cut years are 2005–2019, and the check is ΔF̂ = F̂(x_post) − F̂(x_pre).
  - For any pre-period before 2020, x_pre's legacy block *is the post-cut 2022 landscape*. ΔF̂ then comes only from the clock features. The legacy-feature part of the habitat function is attenuated and never checked.
  - A's T5 estimates the age curve directly from clock bins, so it is far less exposed.
- **Severity: MAJOR (shared by all five).**
- **Fix:** restrict legacy-block rows to ≥ 2020 (or mark the block missing before 2020 and let the clock carry 2016–19). In panel checks, compute ΔF̂ with a clock-only sub-model.

## Most serious unaddressed flaw per design

Beyond the shared LANDFIRE-vintage flaw above:

- **A: MAJOR.** The support weight π is computed as a k-NN density in **covariate** space (a 10-d PCA). The off-trail problem A sets out to solve is **geographic**: birders walk the logging road *through* the same young cut a hunter works off-trail. Footprint covariates of those trail checklists look like off-trail young cover, so π ≈ 1 there and the residual keeps full authority. A's central differentiator therefore does not switch on where A claims it matters. The T2 cross uses "unvisited cells", not "covariate-novel cells", so it would not catch this. A is also the heaviest build (habitat cube, PDAL lidar, alternating L-BFGS/GBM/GAM, support model, panel) for one owner in mid-season.
- **B: MEDIUM.** Every gate is decided on fall-only HH and Holm-adjusted across about six gates. HH is about 20% of checklists, and only about 15% of detections are in fall. G1 "stop the checklist programme" can therefore false-kill a working model. B states no minimum detectable effect (MDE) for fall-only G1 before committing to that kill rule (A raised the same issue against E).
- **C: MEDIUM.** The infrastructure mask removes everything within 30 m of every OSM track, path and skid road.
  - In northern-forest grouse country, gated logging roads and grown-in skid trails are **real** fall habitat (gravel, clover, edge, brood use). They are also the routes hunters walk.
  - In heavily roaded Maine industrial land, the mask deletes much of the young-cut signal. The embedding block it protects has P(pass) = 0.30 and contributes little to a hunter.
  - C spends its complexity budget (AlphaEarth, transport, back-test) on the least hunter-relevant lever.
- **D: MAJOR.** The primary kill metric is **all-season** same-day CC. Same-day pairs ≥ 1 km apart are mostly spring roadside hops (C's point), so a drumming map can pass. The fallback ranks by F + D_vis, but the "visual" codes (FL, DD, NY, CF, "on road") are June–July brood and gravel observations, not October cover (A's point, which D never answered). The fallback can therefore promote road-edge brood coverts.
- **E: MEDIUM.** The T1 kill rule uses **AND**: it kills only if CC fails *and* the fall TkL₅ CI includes 0. A model can therefore ship on a spring-driven CC win with an inconclusive fall result. H_fall is fitted on the thin fall sample with legacy features that are vintage-mismatched before 2020 (see above). Fall eBird visual detections also over-represent roadside gravelling birds, and nothing de-biases H_fall for that.

## Ranking

1. E
2. B
3. D
4. A
5. C

**1st: E (FLUSH-E).** E is the design most likely to put a hunter in front of more October flushes.
- It is the only one that fits an **independent, unshrunk fall-only model**. That is the right response to fall dispersal and to the 61% spring label skew, and it is a test that can genuinely fail, which a shrunk fall head cannot.
- It is the lightest to build, about 5 GB of one-row LightGBM, so the owner can actually run it in October.
- It ships a usable covert card with access classes, peak window and freshness. Its rule-out layer saves wasted walking, and its back-tested forecast mode is honest about "rising" coverts.
- Its ecological content is thinner than A's. Adopting A's guild set and v0 prior fixes that cheaply.

**Last: C (FLUSH-C).** C's X2 panel is a good idea (a direct test that a cut becomes a covert as it ages), but every design now shares it. C's own additions point away from the hunter:
- a gated satellite-embedding block with a 30% prior of admission;
- an infrastructure mask that removes the logging roads and skid trails that are both fall grouse habitat and the hunter's walking routes;
- forecast transport machinery.

On top of that, its X2 model check suffers most from the pre-2022 LANDFIRE vintage gap, because the "pre" landscape is read from 2022.

## Elements the winner (E) should adopt

1. **From A:** the zero-fit v0 ecological prior map in place of bare H250. It uses independent weak guild priors, including **alder/shrub wetland, old field and spruce–fir regeneration**, a separate softwood age curve, unimodal conifer share, and additive understory under older canopy (shelterwood). Also adopt A's hunting-pressure term ξ·press in the hunt-log model.
2. **From B:** decide *every* gate on fall HH checklists (B6), but with an MDE computed first. This replaces E's lenient AND kill rule.
